Control map / Contain

Control · General verb · Contain

How do we hold what wants to escape?

Air presses in, steam pushes out, heat leaks, plasma would melt any wall. Containing means building a boundary strong or clever enough to keep things where we want them.

Start here: a plastic bottle on a flight

Drink from a plastic bottle at cruising altitude, close the cap, and look at it after landing: it has crumpled. Nobody squeezed it. What did?

Pause and answer
  1. What was the cabin pressure when you closed the bottle?
  2. What pressure was outside the bottle after landing?
  3. What would it take to stop the bottle crumpling?
Compare your reasoning

Aircraft cabins are pressurised below sea-level pressure. Air sealed at cabin pressure ends up lower than the sea-level air outside, so the outside pushes the walls in. A stiffer bottle, a metal can or a valve would resist. Every Contain verb is the same contest: a pressure, heat or substance on one side, a boundary on the other.

01

The everyday case, worked

You are already contained: inside an ocean of air ten tonnes per square metre heavy.

Worked: the air on a square metre

101,325 Pa ÷ 9.806 65 m/s² ≈ 10,332 kg/m² (NIST). Every square metre of ground and skin carries about ten tonnes of air. A sealed tin survives because the gas inside pushes back.

02

The mechanisms

Strength holds pressure; emptiness holds heat; fields hold what walls cannot.

  • Strong walls hold pressure: cookers, boilers, submarine hulls.
  • Vacuum and insulation hold heat in or out: flasks and cryogenic rocket tanks (see Boil).
  • Magnetic fields hold what touches nothing: plasma in tokamaks, antimatter in traps.

The idea of air pressure itself was contained first: Evangelista Torricelli described his mercury barometer in a letter of 11 June 1644 (Museo Galileo), and on 19 September 1648 Florin Périer, for Blaise Pascal, carried one up the Puy de Dôme and saw the column fall (Clermont libraries).

Worked: pressure at Challenger Deep

Taking the depth as about 10.9 km and seawater at about 1,025 kg/m³ (both assumed round values, TBC against a sourced figure): 1,025 × 9.81 × 10,900 ≈ 110 MPa, about 1,080 times normal sea-level pressure.

04

The record, and how far it goes

Holding something hotter than the Sun’s core without touching it.

Record: France’s WEST tokamak held a plasma for 1,337 seconds (about 22 minutes) on 12 February 2025 (CEA), beating EAST’s 1,066 s from January 2025 (HFIPS). The two records are for different plasma conditions; compare with care.

How far from normal?

No material wall survives plasma at fusion temperatures; tungsten melts at 3,422 °C (see Heat). Fusion plasmas at around 100 million °C are some 350,000 times normal temperature in kelvin, held by magnetic fields rather than walls.

05

Limits

Containment is never perfect, only good enough for long enough.

  • Pressure vessels fail when walls thin or valves stick: safety valves exist for this.
  • Heat leaks through every insulator; cryogenic fuel boils off.
  • Magnetic bottles leak particles and energy, which is why plasma records are in seconds and minutes.
02 · Distance from normal

How far the records go from normal

Contain: about 1,080 atmospheres, and plasma at 350,000 times normal

On a normal day we live under about 101 kPa. Trieste’s hull held about 1,080 times that at Challenger Deep (worked, depth assumed). Tokamaks now hold plasma at fusion temperatures for over 20 minutes.

03 · Review

Check your reasoning

Answer each one in your notebook before you open it.

Why did the bottle crumple after landing?

It was sealed at the lower cabin pressure; after landing the higher sea-level pressure outside pushed it in.

What did Pascal’s 1648 mountain experiment show?

The mercury column fell as the barometer was carried up, so the column was held up by the weight of the air above it.

Why can’t a fusion reactor use a strong metal wall to hold its plasma?

At around 100 million °C no material survives contact; magnetic fields hold the plasma away from the walls.
