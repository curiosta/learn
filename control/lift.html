Control · Move · Lift

Why does lifting a bucket feel hard when it takes so little energy?

Lifting is work against gravity: weight times height. The energy is surprisingly small; what is hard is producing the force, holding it safely, and doing it fast. From a well bucket to a 20,000-tonne crane lift, the formula never changes.

Start here: carrying water upstairs

Carry a 10-litre bucket of water up one floor and your arms and legs ache. Yet a phone charger delivers more energy than that in a few minutes.

Pause and answer
  1. How much energy does the bucket gain by going up 3 m?
  2. Why does it still feel so tiring?
  3. What would make the job easier without changing the energy?
Compare your reasoning

The bucket gains 10 kg × 9.81 × 3 m ≈ 294 J (NIST): less than 0.1 Wh. It feels hard because muscles are inefficient and you must hold up the weight the whole time, and your own body goes up the stairs too. A pulley or a pump does not reduce the energy; it lets a smaller force act over a longer distance, or lets a motor supply the force.

Where the value is

Where is the value in lifting?

Lifting itself takes very little energy per kilogram; the value lies in what the height makes possible, water flowing to fields or a roof tank, a ship’s module set in place. Working: how small the energy is

Raising 1 kg by 1 m stores m g h = 1 × 9.80665 × 1 ≈ 9.81 J ≈ 0.0027 Wh (NIST). So 1 kWh (3.6 MJ) can lift one tonne by 3,600,000 ÷ (1,000 × 9.80665) ≈ 367 m, if nothing is lost. A 1,000-litre roof tank 10 m up needs at least 1,000 × 9.80665 × 10 ≈ 98 kJ ≈ 0.027 kWh. At an assumed ₹8 per kWh that is about ₹0.22 a fill before pump losses. Your real tariff is TBC.

What you can do with this

  • Home habit: measure your pump against the ideal

    Row 1: the ideal energy for a 1,000-litre fill at 10 m is 0.027 kWh. Note your motor’s rated power and how long it runs to fill the tank: kW × hours is what you actually use. The ratio shows how much is lost to friction and an oversized or worn pump. Typical home-pump efficiency is TBC.

  • Small business: in lifting, the machine costs more than the energy

    Row 3: the energy per kg is tiny, so the cost of a lift is mostly the crane, the crew and the time: a fixed cost spread over each job, not a variable cost. Price a lifting job by the hour and the risk, not by the kilowatt-hour.

  • Skill: rigging and lifting safety

    Every row depends on loads held safely: slings, brakes and safety catches like the one Otis demonstrated in 1854 (Guinness). Rigging is a trade in its own right; where certified training is offered locally is TBC.

01

The physics in plain words

Lifting energy depends only on mass, gravity and height.

The energy stored by lifting a mass m through height h is m g h, with standard gravity g = 9.80665 m/s² (NIST). The path does not matter: a ramp, a pulley or a crane all store the same energy for the same height. They change the force you need, not the work.

Worked: lifting energy at a glance

Per kilogram per metre: 9.81 J ≈ 0.0027 Wh. A 70 kg person (assumed) climbing 3 m: 70 × 9.81 × 3 ≈ 2.1 kJ. Water 118 m up at Gayatri: 9.81 × 118 ≈ 1.16 kJ per kg.

03

The climb

Height is the first step of every journey up.

  • Buildings. Elisha Graves Otis demonstrated his safety elevator at New York’s Crystal Palace in 1854: halfway through, he had the cable cut and the ratchet held. The first permanent installation followed in 1857 (Guinness).
  • Farming. The Gayatri pumphouse lifts 622 m³ of water a second through 118 m in Telangana (MEIL); see Pump.
  • Shipyards. The Taisun crane at Yantai Raffles Shipyard, China, lifted 20,133 tonnes on 18 April 2008 (Guinness).
  • Space. ISRO’s heaviest launcher, GSLV Mk III (LVM3), first flew on 5 June 2017 (ISRO). Reaching orbit is mostly about speed, not height; see Launch.
04

The record, and how far it goes

A barge full of water, raised by a single crane.

Record: the heaviest weight lifted by a crane is 20,133 tonnes, a ballasted barge, raised by the Taisun crane at Yantai Raffles Shipyard on 18 April 2008 (Guinness World Records).

Worked: distance from normal

On a normal day people lift loads of a few to tens of kilograms. Against a 10 kg bucket, Taisun’s lift was 20,133,000 ÷ 10 ≈ 2 million times heavier. Gravity itself barely changes on Earth’s surface; standard gravity is 9.80665 m/s² (NIST).

05

Try it: a pulley from a broom and a rope

Feel the same work with half the force.

You need: a smooth broom handle held firmly between two chairs, a strong rope about 3 m long, and a bag with 2–3 kg of books.

  1. Tie the rope to the bag and lift it straight up by hand: note the effort.
  2. Loop the rope over the broom handle and pull down: the force is about the same, but pulling down is easier because your weight helps.
  3. Tie one end to the broom, pass the rope under a handle on the bag and pull up: you now pull about half the force, but pull twice as much rope.
  4. Work out: is the work (force × distance) different?

Safety

Use a light load and keep feet clear of it. Have an adult hold the chairs steady; never stand under a lifted load.

06

Limits: what stops us

Energy is rarely the limit; materials and safety are.

  • Materials. Cables, booms and hooks must carry the load with a margin; crane capacity is set by structure, not by motor energy.
  • Safety. A falling load is the main danger; Otis’s ratchet made passenger lifts possible (Guinness).
  • Losses. Pumps and motors waste part of their input; at Gayatri about a quarter (our estimate, see Pump).
  • To space, height is not enough. Orbit near the surface needs about 7.9 km/s sideways (worked on Launch).
02 · Distance from normal

How far the records go from normal

Lift: from a 10 kg bucket to a 20,133-tonne barge

On a normal day a person lifts a few kilograms through a few metres, a few hundred joules. Engineering lifts rivers 118 m at 622 tonnes a second and, in one record lift on 18 April 2008, a 20,133-tonne barge: about 2 million times a bucket.

03 · Review

Check your reasoning

Answer each one in your notebook before you open it.

Does a ramp reduce the energy needed to raise a load?

No. It reduces the force but increases the distance; m g h is the same.

About how high can 1 kWh lift a tonne?

About 367 m, with no losses.

Why do lifting businesses price by the hour rather than by energy?

The energy is tiny; the costs are the machine, the crew, the time and the risk.

What did Otis’s 1854 demonstration show?

That a ratchet safety catch could stop an elevator falling if its cable were cut.
