Control · Convert · Heat

How hot can we make things?

Heating is converting: electricity, fuel or motion turned into the jiggling of atoms. Start with a kettle, climb through molten iron and the Sun’s core, and end at about 5.5 trillion kelvin.

Start here: the kettle

Fill an electric kettle with one litre of tap water and switch it on. In a few minutes it roars, then clicks off. Where did the energy come from, where did it go, and why does a full kettle take longer than a half-full one?

Pause and answer
  1. What does “hotter” mean for the water molecules themselves?
  2. Why would twice the water need twice the time?
  3. Is there a top limit to how hot you could make something?
Compare your reasoning

Temperature measures how hard the particles of a substance are jiggling. The kettle’s element turns electrical energy into that jiggling. Each kilogram of water needs about 4.18 kJ for each degree it warms, so twice the water needs twice the energy and, at the same power, twice the time. There is no known top limit: add energy fast enough and atoms lose their electrons (plasma), then nuclei melt into their quarks. The rest of this page climbs that ladder.

Where the value is

Which kind of heating earns the most per unit of energy?

Heat turns cheap matter into dearer matter: ore into steel, quartz into silicon, flour into bread. Below we rank five heating processes by value added per kilowatt-hour of energy. Value added here means the selling price minus the main material input: a rough gross margin before energy, labour, rent and the plant itself. The prices are dated, and Indian prices are used where we found them.

Where this sits on the value quadrant: Form. Heat adds value mostly by changing what the matter is (ore to steel, dough to bread), not by when or where it is sold. See the quadrant.

What you can do with this

  • Small business: price a loaf like a founder

    Row 1 has the highest value per kWh and needs the smallest plant, which is why home bakeries exist. Work out your unit cost: flour, oil, yeast, packaging and the oven’s kWh for one batch. Set a selling price and compute your contribution margin per loaf. Then find your break-even: how many loaves a month cover the oven and the rent? Check local food-safety registration rules before you sell.

  • Skill: track a spread every month

    Row 4’s margin is the gap between the HRC price and the iron-ore price. Both are published: the World Bank Pink Sheet each month and BigMint each week. Note the two prices for three months and recompute ₹ per kWh. You will see that a steel plant’s margin moves with prices it does not control. That is the point to remember before calling any business a moat.

  • Think about scale

    Polysilicon (row 3) earns about ₹456 per kg but only about ₹8 per kWh. It pays only for plants that run huge volumes on cheap electricity. That is scalability, and it is why a home bakery and a polysilicon plant are opposite kinds of business.

01

The physics in plain words

To heat is to convert some other energy into random motion of particles.

Heat flows from hot to cold. To make something hotter you must deliver energy faster than it leaks away. How much you need depends on the material’s specific heat: water takes about 4,184 J per kg per °C, copper only about 385 (USGS). That is why a copper pan heats quickly and a pot of water slowly.

Three ways to deliver it: pass a current through a resistance (the kettle), burn a fuel (see Burn, planned), or compress or collide matter (the fusion and collider records below).

02

A worked kettle

A number you can check with a watch.

Worked: one litre, 25 → 100 °C

Energy: 1 kg × 4.18 kJ/(kg·°C) × 75 °C ≈ 314 kJ (specific heat from USGS).

Time, if the element delivers 2 kW and nothing is lost: 314,000 J ÷ 2,000 W ≈ 157 s ≈ 2.6 min.

Assumption: 2 kW is a typical rating we assumed, not a sourced value. Read the rating plate on your own kettle and redo the sum.

Real kettles take a little longer because some heat warms the kettle body and the air. The gap between your measured time and this sum is your kettle’s loss.

India on the ladder. ISRO’s Crew module Atmospheric Re-entry Experiment (CARE), flown on 18 December 2014, tested the ablative heat shield a crewed capsule needs to survive re-entry heating (ISRO). Heat shields and fusion magnets solve the same problem from opposite sides: keep extreme heat where you want it.

Heat that pays its way. On 5 December 2022 the National Ignition Facility heated a fuel capsule with 2.05 MJ of laser energy and got 3.15 MJ of fusion energy out, the first lab ignition (LLNL).

04

The record, and how far it goes

The hottest matter ever made existed for far less than a blink.

Record: ALICE, at CERN’s Large Hadron Collider, announced on 13 August 2012 that lead-ion collisions had reached over 5 trillion K, perhaps 5.5 trillion K (Guinness World Records). The earlier RHIC result of about 4 trillion °C (2010) survived “for much less than a billionth of a trillionth of a second” (BNL).

Worked: distance from normal

5.5 × 10¹² K ÷ 288 K ≈ 1.9 × 10¹⁰. The record is about 19 billion times Earth’s normal surface temperature, measured from absolute zero. Nature’s hottest surface reading, 56.7 °C, is only 1.15 times normal.

Two kinds of “hottest”

Temperature records come in two flavours: how hot, and how long. Colliders win “how hot” for a vanishing instant. Tokamaks such as KSTAR win “how long at fusion temperatures” (100 million °C ions for 48 s). Compare like with like.

05

Try it: measure your kettle

Your own number beats ours.

You need: a kettle with a rating plate, a measuring jug, a phone stopwatch, and a kitchen thermometer if you have one.

  1. Measure exactly one litre of tap water and note its temperature (or assume 25 °C and say so).
  2. Time from switch-on to click-off.
  3. Compute the energy the element delivered: power (W) × time (s).
  4. Divide 314 kJ (or your own sum) by that. That fraction is your kettle’s efficiency.
  5. Repeat with half a litre. Does the time halve? If not, why not?

Safety

Boiling water and steam scald. Do not open the lid while it boils; let an adult handle the pouring.

06

Limits: what stops us

Making heat is easy. Holding it is the hard part.

  • Containers melt. No metal survives above tungsten’s 3,422 °C (PubChem). Hotter work needs magnetic fields (tokamaks), inertia (NIF’s capsule) or a vanishing instant (colliders).
  • Heat leaks. Hot things radiate, and losses climb steeply with temperature; that is why fusion records are quoted in seconds, not hours. EAST held a high-confinement plasma for 1,066 s in January 2025 (reported 21 January) (HFIPS, CAS); WEST held a plasma for 1,337 s on 12 February 2025 (CEA).
  • Bodies are fragile. A human core above 40 °C is an emergency (MedlinePlus). See Normal on Earth.
02 · Distance from normal

How far the records go from normal

Heat: about 19 billion times normal

Normal is 288 K. Nature’s hottest surface air reached 1.15 times that. The hottest matter people have made, about 5.5 trillion K at CERN in 2012, is about 1.9 × 10¹⁰ times normal. The longest-held fusion-hot plasma (KSTAR ions at 100 million °C, 48 s) is about 350,000 times normal (worked: 1.0 × 10⁸ K ÷ 288 K).

03 · Review

Check your reasoning

Answer each one in your notebook before you open it.

A copper pan and a pot of water get the same heat. Which warms faster, and why?

The copper pan. Copper needs about 385 J/(kg·°C), water about 4,184, so the same energy raises copper’s temperature about eleven times as much per kilogram.

Why is the kettle’s top temperature about 100 °C no matter how long you leave it on?

Once water boils, extra energy goes into turning liquid into steam (latent heat), not into raising temperature. The ceiling is set by pressure; see Boil.

Why do colliders hold the hottest-temperature record but tokamaks are the path to fusion power?

A collider’s fireball lasts a vanishing fraction of a second. Power needs heat held long enough for fusion to pay back the energy put in, which is the tokamak and NIF problem.

How many times hotter than normal is the Sun’s core?

About 15 million °C ≈ 1.5 × 10⁷ K; ÷ 288 K ≈ 52,000 times.
