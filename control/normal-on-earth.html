Control · The baseline

What is normal on Earth’s surface?

Before we can say how hot, cold, fast or tightly held a record is, we need the ruler. This page is that ruler: the pressure, temperature, gravity and air you were born into, and how far a human body can stray from them.

Start here: a sealed packet of chips

Buy a sealed packet of chips in Chennai and carry it up to a hill station, or onto a flight. It puffs up like a pillow. Nothing went into the packet. So what changed?

Pause and answer
  1. What is pushing on the outside of the packet at sea level?
  2. What happens to that push as you go higher?
  3. If the packet were your body, what would be the same and what would be different?
Compare your reasoning

The air outside presses on every square metre with about 101 kilopascals at sea level, the weight of the air above you. Higher up there is less air above, so the outside push falls while the air sealed inside the packet keeps its old pressure, and it bulges. Your body does the same sum all day. Your lungs and blood are tuned to the pressure, temperature and oxygen of the surface. This page measures that “normal”, so every other Control page can say how far from it its records go.

01

Pressure: the weight of the air

You live at the bottom of an ocean of air about 8.5 km “deep” in scale height.

Air has weight. The column above each square metre of sea-level ground pushes down with a standard 101,325 pascals (NIST, exact by definition). NASA’s fact sheet gives an average surface pressure of 1,014 millibars and an atmospheric scale height of 8.5 km: climb about that far and the pressure falls to roughly a third (NASA).

Worked: how heavy is the air on you?

101,325 Pa ÷ 9.806 65 m/s² ≈ 10,300 kg per m². Every square metre carries about ten tonnes of air. You do not feel it because the fluids in your body push back with the same pressure.

So weather moves sea-level pressure by under ±15%. Climbing to the top of the world takes away two-thirds of it.

02

Temperature: a narrow band

In kelvin, from absolute zero, the whole range of Earth’s weather is a thin slice.

NASA gives Earth’s average surface temperature as 288 K (15 °C), with a typical day–night swing of about 283–293 K (NASA). The kelvin starts at absolute zero, where thermal motion is at its minimum (BIPM), which is why we compare records in kelvin.

Surface air temperature, normal and the natural extremes
MeasureValueSource
Global average surface288 K (15 °C)NASA
Hottest recorded, Furnace Creek, Death Valley, 10 Jul 191356.7 °C (329.9 K)WMO
Coldest recorded, Vostok, Antarctica, 21 Jul 1983−89.2 °C (184 K)WMO; Turner et al.
Worked: how wide is normal?

Hottest ÷ average: 329.9 K ÷ 288 K ≈ 1.15. Coldest ÷ average: 184 K ÷ 288 K ≈ 0.64. Nature’s whole surface range sits within about −36% to +15% of normal, measured from absolute zero.

Without its atmosphere, a planet at Earth’s distance with Earth’s reflectivity (Bond albedo 0.294) would radiate at a black-body temperature of 254 K (NASA). The roughly 34 K gap to 288 K is the greenhouse effect at work.

03

Gravity and the speed to leave

Everything you lift, pump or launch is measured against this one pull.

Standard gravity is defined as 9.806 65 m/s² (NIST). The real pull varies a little: about 9.780 at the equator and 9.832 at the poles (NASA). To leave Earth without further push you need 11.186 km/s, the escape velocity (NASA).

Worked: energy to escape, per kilogram

½ × (11,186 m/s)² ≈ 62.6 MJ/kg. That is about 200 times the energy needed to bring a litre of water from 25 °C to the boil (see Heat).

04

Air and sunlight

The air you breathe and the light you live by have measured, steady values.

Dry air near the surface is 78.08% nitrogen, 20.95% oxygen and 0.934% argon (9,340 ppm), with about 420 ppm of carbon dioxide; water vapour is highly variable, around 1% (NASA). Air density at the surface is about 1.217 kg/m³. Sunlight arrives at the top of the atmosphere at about 1,361 W/m² (NASA), the energy income every Convert page starts from.

05

Water: where normal boils and freezes

The boiling point of water is not a fixed number. It is a pressure reading.

At 101.325 kPa water boils at 99.97 °C on the modern IAPWS-95 scale (NIST WebBook). At Everest’s 33.7 kPa it boils at about 72 °C (worked from the same NIST table), which is why food cooks slowly up high. Its specific heat is about 4,184 J per kg per °C (USGS), one of the highest of common substances. The full story is on Boil.

The human envelope

Your body survives a band of conditions far narrower than the planet’s.

The body runs on about 100 W at rest and keeps its skin at or below about 35 °C so heat can flow out (Sherwood & Huber). If the air is so hot and humid that sweat cannot evaporate (a wet-bulb near skin temperature), the heat has nowhere to go. That is why the wet-bulb limit sits close to 35 °C.

Try it: see normal pressure move

Seal an empty plastic bottle at the top of a building, a hill or before a flight lands, then open it at the bottom. It crumples a little on the way down. You have just measured the change in the weight of the air with a bottle.

02 · Distance from normal

How far the records go from normal

This page is the ruler

Every Control page states its records as a distance from these values: 288 K for temperature, 101.325 kPa for pressure, 1 g for gravity, 11.186 km/s as the speed that leaves gravity behind, and the 37 °C core of a human body. Nature’s own surface extremes sit within about 0.64× to 1.15× of normal temperature, and about ±15% of normal sea-level pressure. Human records go billions of times further: see Heat (about 19 billion times hotter) and Cool (about 7.6 trillion times colder).

03 · Review

Check your reasoning

Answer each one in your notebook before you open it.

Why does a sealed chip packet swell on a mountain road but not in a hot car?

On the mountain, the outside pressure falls while the inside stays the same. In a hot car the outside pressure is unchanged; the warmer air inside pushes a little harder, but the change is small compared with losing a large share of the atmosphere.

Which is further from normal in kelvin: Vostok’s −89.2 °C or Death Valley’s 56.7 °C?

Vostok. 184 K is 36% below 288 K; 329.9 K is 15% above it.

Why does dal take longer to cook at a hill station?

Lower pressure lowers the boiling point, so the water cannot get as hot before it boils away. On Everest it boils at about 72 °C.

Why can a wet-bulb temperature of 35 °C kill even in shade with water to drink?

Your skin must stay a little below core temperature to lose the 100 W you make at rest. If even a wet surface cannot cool below about 35 °C, heat builds up inside you.
