Control map / Regulate

Control · General verb · Regulate

How do we hold things steady?

Regulating is the art of staying the same: measure, compare with a target, correct. Your body does it every second; clocks, air-conditioners, pacemakers and space stations copy the trick.

Start here: your own thermostat

Walk from an air-conditioned room into the midday sun, and back. Your skin flushes, you sweat, you shiver a little in the cold. Your core stays at about 37 °C throughout. How does your body know what to do?

Pause and answer
  1. What does your body have to measure?
  2. What does it compare the measurement with?
  3. What does it do to correct an error, in heat and in cold?
Compare your reasoning

Your body senses temperature, compares it with a set point near 37 °C, and acts: sweating and widening skin blood vessels to lose heat, shivering and narrowing them to keep it. That loop of measure, compare and correct is feedback. Every Regulate verb, from a fridge thermostat to the ISS life-support system, is a feedback loop.

01

The everyday case

Look around the kitchen: the regulators are everywhere.

Your body holds about 37 °C; below 35 °C is hypothermia (NHS) and above 40 °C heat stroke (MedlinePlus). A fridge thermostat switches the compressor on and off to hold a set temperature (see Cool). A pressure cooker’s weight lifts to hold its pressure, and so its temperature, steady (see Boil).

02

The mechanisms

Every regulator has a sensor, a set point and an actuator.

Christiaan Huygens patented the pendulum clock on 16 June 1657 (DBNL): the pendulum’s steady swing regulated the gears. Willis Carrier’s air-conditioning design of 17 July 1902 regulated temperature and humidity for a printing works (Willis Carrier). Medicine learned to regulate the body itself: insulin was first injected into a patient, Leonard Thompson, on 11 January 1922 (University of Toronto), and the first implanted pacemaker went in on 8 October 1958 (PMC).

04

The record, and how far it goes

The biggest fridge in the world holds a ring 27 km round colder than space.

CERN’s cryogenic system holds about 36,000 tonnes of magnet cold mass at 1.9 K, colder than outer space at 2.7 K, using 40 MW of electricity and 120 tonnes of helium (CERN). Whether that is formally a world record is TBC; we have not found a records body that lists it.

Worked: distance from normal

288 K ÷ 1.9 K ≈ 150: the magnets are held about 150 times colder than normal, steadily, for months at a time.

05

Limits

Every regulator overshoots a little and costs energy.

  • Delays make regulators overshoot and oscillate.
  • Cost rises steeply with tightness: cooling to 1.9 K costs about 160 J of work per joule removed even in theory (see Cool).
  • Bodies fail when the load beats the regulator: humid heat near a 35 °C wet-bulb (see Normal on Earth).
02 · Distance from normal

How far the records go from normal

Regulate: about 150 times colder than normal, held steady

Your body regulates itself within a few degrees of 37 °C on a normal day. The ISS recycles 98% of its water; the LHC holds 36,000 tonnes about 150 times colder than normal.

03 · Review

Check your reasoning

Answer each one in your notebook before you open it.

What are the three parts of any feedback regulator?

A sensor that measures, a set point to compare with, and an actuator that corrects.

Why is a pressure cooker a temperature regulator?

By holding the pressure, the weight holds the boiling point, and so the water’s temperature.

Why does tighter regulation cost more?

Smaller errors need faster, finer correction; for cold, each degree closer to zero costs more work.
