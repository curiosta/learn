Business words, plainly

The money words behind “where the value is”

Every Control page ranks what energy is worth. These are the words founders and investors use for that kind of sum, each in one line with a source. This page explains words only. It is not business, investment or financial advice.

Back to the Control map

Cost and price

Unit economics
Profit or loss measured on the smallest unit of the business: one product sold or one customer served. Source: Corporate Finance Institute
Unit cost (cost per unit)
The total cost of making one unit: fixed and variable costs added together, divided by the number of units made. Source: Investopedia
Variable cost
A cost that rises and falls with how much you make, such as flour for each loaf. Source: Investopedia
Fixed cost
A cost that stays the same whatever you make, such as rent. Source: Investopedia
Cost of goods sold (COGS)
The direct cost of the goods you actually sold: materials and the labour that made them. Source: Investopedia
Markup
The amount added to cost to reach the selling price, usually given as a percentage of cost. Source: Investopedia
Value added
The extra worth a step creates. On these pages: the selling price minus the main inputs bought in. Source: Investopedia

Margins and survival

Gross margin
Revenue minus cost of goods sold, often shown as a percentage of revenue. Source: Investopedia
Contribution margin
Selling price per unit minus variable cost per unit: what each sale contributes towards fixed costs and profit. Source: Investopedia
Break-even
The point where total revenue equals total cost, so there is neither profit nor loss. Source: Investopedia
Working capital
Current assets minus current liabilities: the cash cushion for day-to-day running. Source: Investopedia
Burn rate
How fast a company that is not yet profitable spends its cash, usually per month. Source: Investopedia
Runway
How many months the cash will last at the current burn rate. Source: Corporate Finance Institute

Customers

Customer acquisition cost (CAC)
Total sales and marketing spending divided by the number of new customers it won. Source: Corporate Finance Institute
Lifetime value (LTV)
The total profit a business expects from one customer over the whole relationship. Source: Corporate Finance Institute
LTV/CAC ratio
Lifetime value divided by acquisition cost; it shows whether winning a customer pays back. Source: Corporate Finance Institute

Growth and protection

Scalability
The ability to grow output and revenue without costs growing as fast. Source: Investopedia
Moat (economic moat)
A lasting advantage that protects a business’s profits from competitors. Source: Corporate Finance Institute
USP (unique selling point)
The feature or benefit that makes a product different from, and better than, its competitors. Source: Cambridge Dictionary
Arbitrage
Buying in one market and selling in another to profit from a price difference. Strict arbitrage is near riskless; the time and place spreads on these pages carry real costs, losses and risk. Source: Investopedia

Money for a business

Valuation
The process of estimating what a business or an asset is worth. Source: Investopedia
Equity
Ownership: what is left for the owners after all debts are subtracted from the assets. Source: Investopedia
Royalty
A payment to an owner for the ongoing use of something they own, such as a patent or a brand, often a percentage of sales. Source: Investopedia
Debt
Borrowed money that must be repaid, usually with interest. Source: Investopedia
