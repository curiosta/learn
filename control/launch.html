Control · Move · Launch

How hard must you throw to leave Earth?

Everything you throw comes back, unless you throw it fast enough. Launching is moving against gravity at speeds that start with a cricket ball and end, so far, at 192 kilometres per second.

  • ≈7.9 km/sTo orbit just above the ground (worked)NASA radius, g
  • 11.186 km/sEarth’s escape velocityNASA

Start here: a ball thrown straight up

Throw a ball straight up. It slows, stops, and falls back to your hand. Throw harder and it goes higher, but it always returns. Is there a speed at which it would never come back?

Pause and answer
  1. What is slowing the ball on the way up?
  2. If you could throw sideways fast enough, what would happen as the ground curved away beneath it?
  3. Why might it help to launch from near the equator?
Compare your reasoning

Gravity takes 9.81 m/s off the ball’s speed every second. Throw sideways fast enough and the ball falls around the curve of the Earth without hitting it: that is orbit, about 7.9 km/s near the surface. Throw faster still, 11.186 km/s, and gravity can never pull it back: escape velocity. Launching east near the equator borrows some of Earth’s own spin. A rocket is simply a machine for reaching those speeds.

Working

Kinetic energy per kg is ½ v². Orbit: 0.5 × 7,900² ≈ 31.2 MJ ≈ 8.7 kWh. Escape: 0.5 × 11,186² ≈ 62.6 MJ ≈ 17.4 kWh. Real launches need more than this, because the rocket must also lift its own fuel, climb and push through air. Compare lifting: 1 kWh lifts a tonne 367 m (see Lift).

01

The physics in plain words

A rocket throws mass backwards very fast, and the reaction throws the rocket forwards.

Launch means beating gravity. Gravity pulls with 9.806 65 m/s² at standard sea level (NIST). A rocket burns propellant and throws the exhaust out of the nozzle; by Newton’s third law the rocket is pushed the other way. To lift off at all, its thrust must beat its weight.

Worked: three speeds

Ball to 10 m: v = √(2 × 9.81 × 10) ≈ 14 m/s.

Orbit skimming the surface (no air, radius 6,371 km from NASA): v = √(g × R) = √(9.81 × 6.371 × 10⁶) ≈ 7.9 km/s. Real orbits are a little higher and a little slower.

Escape: 11.186 km/s ({c("nasa_fact","NASA")}), which carries ½ × 11,186² ≈ 62.6 MJ of energy per kilogram.

03

The record, and how far it goes

The fastest thing people have built is falling towards the Sun.

Record: on 24 December 2024 Parker Solar Probe passed 6.1 million km from the Sun’s surface at about 430,000 mph (≈692,000 km/h, ≈192 km/s), the fastest human-made object (NASA). It got that speed mostly by falling towards the Sun, after seven Venus flybys bent its orbit ever closer, not from its rocket alone.

Worked: distance from normal

192 km/s ÷ 11.186 km/s ≈ 17. Parker moves about 17 times Earth’s escape velocity. Against a ball thrown to 10 m (14 m/s), it is about 192,000 ÷ 14 ≈ 14,000 times faster.

Like with like

Parker’s speed is measured relative to the Sun, not to Earth’s surface. The useful comparison for “launching from Earth” is the escape velocity, 11.186 km/s, which several deep-space missions have exceeded since Luna 1.

04

Try it: a bottle rocket on paper

Time a throw and work out its speed.

You need: a ball, an open space, and a phone stopwatch.

  1. Throw the ball straight up and time how long until it lands back in your hand.
  2. Half that time is the climb. Launch speed ≈ 9.81 × (half the time). A 3-second round trip means about 15 m/s.
  3. Height ≈ ½ × 9.81 × (half the time)². Does that match how high it looked?
  4. How many of your throws would add up to 11.186 km/s?
05

Limits: what stops us

Most of a rocket is fuel to lift the fuel.

  • Fuel lifts fuel. Each extra unit of speed needs exponentially more propellant, so rockets drop empty stages. Cryogenic fuels give more speed per kilogram but must be kept at 20–90 K (see Boil).
  • Air heats you. Coming back through the atmosphere needs a heat shield, which ISRO tested with CARE in 2014 (ISRO). See Heat.
  • Bodies have limits. Crew need pressure and oxygen at every stage; see Normal on Earth. Sustained g-force limits are TBC.
02 · Distance from normal

How far the records go from normal

Launch: about 17 times escape velocity

On a normal day, everything you throw comes back: gravity is 1 g and the speed to leave is 11.186 km/s. People have built machines that orbit (about 7.9 km/s), escape (since Luna 1 in 1959), and now, with Parker Solar Probe, reach about 192 km/s, some 17 times Earth’s escape velocity.

03 · Review

Check your reasoning

Answer each one in your notebook before you open it.

Why does a ball thrown at 14 m/s rise about 10 m?

Gravity removes 9.81 m/s of speed each second; v² = 2gh gives h = 14² ÷ (2 × 9.81) ≈ 10 m.

Why do launch sites like Sriharikota and Cape Canaveral sit near the coast on the east?

Rockets launch eastward to borrow Earth’s spin, and over the sea so spent stages fall away from people.

Is an orbiting astronaut weightless because there is no gravity?

No. Gravity at the ISS is still strong; the astronaut and station are falling together around the Earth.

How did Parker get so fast?

Mainly by falling towards the Sun, with Venus flybys bending its orbit closer each time.
