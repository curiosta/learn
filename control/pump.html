Control map / Move / Pump

Control · Move · Pump

Why can’t a hand pump suck water up from deeper than 10 m?

A pump moves fluid by making a pressure difference. Suction can only borrow the air’s pressure, so it runs out at about 10 metres; pushing has no such limit. From the heart to a 118-metre lift for a river to a rocket engine, pumping is pressure put to work.

Start here: the village hand pump

A hand pump on a shallow well gushes. On a deep borewell the same handle brings up nothing, and a motor is lowered down the bore instead. The handle is the same; the water is just further down.

Pause and answer
  1. When you work the handle, what actually pushes the water up the pipe?
  2. What would happen if the pipe were 20 m tall?
  3. Why does a pump at the bottom of the well not have this problem?
Compare your reasoning

Suction does not pull water. It removes air from the pipe, and the atmosphere pushing on the well’s surface pushes the water up. That push is only 101,325 Pa (NIST), enough to hold up about 10.33 m of water. A submersible pump at the bottom pushes water up, and can make as much pressure as its motor allows.

Where the value is

Where does pumping pay per unit of energy?

Pumping does not change water; it changes where water is, and how high. We could source the energy of a giant lift and the price of piped water in Delhi, but not both for the same water. So the ranking below is partial, and the per-kWh figure is an illustration, not a measurement.

Where this sits on the value quadrant: Space mainly: a pump moves fluid to where it is needed, uphill or across a city. Time enters when water is lifted into a tank to be used later. See the quadrant.

Working: the lift energy and the illustration

Ideal energy to lift 1 m³ (1,000 kg) of water 118 m: 1,000 × 9.80665 × 118 ≈ 1.157 MJ ≈ 0.32 kWh, using standard gravity (NIST). Installed: 7 × 139 MW = 973 MW for 622 m³/s, so 973 ÷ 622 ≈ 1.56 MJ per m³ ≈ 0.43 kWh per m³ (MEIL). 0.32 ÷ 0.43 ≈ 74%: an upper-bound efficiency, if every unit runs at full power (our estimate). Illustration: Delhi’s cheapest domestic rate is ₹5.27 per kL; 5.27 ÷ 0.434 ≈ ₹12 per kWh of lift, and at ₹43.93 per kL ≈ ₹101. Delhi’s water is not lifted by Gayatri; this only shows how a price compares with a lift’s energy. The Delhi tariff is the 2018 notification; any later revision is TBC.

What you can do with this

  • Home habit: read your water meter against the slabs

    Row 1: in the 2018 Delhi tariff the rate per kL jumps five-fold above 20 kL a month (₹5.27 to ₹26.36), and a sewer charge of 60% of the water charge is added (DJB). Reading the meter weekly shows whether a leak or a habit is pushing you over a slab.

  • Skill: pump selection and servicing

    Row 2 shows even a new giant station loses about a quarter of its input power (our estimate). Choosing the right pump for the head, and keeping it serviced, keeps the variable cost of every litre down, from a farm borewell to a housing society.

    Who hires for this and which fairs to watch: NEST Collective → Energy & utilities field work

  • Small business: compare moving by pipe with moving by truck

    Row 3 and the mode table below: a pipeline moves fluid for about ₹2 per tonne-km, below road’s ₹3.6 in the same NITI study. Pipes are a big fixed cost up front; trucks are mostly variable cost. The right choice depends on volume.

01

The physics in plain words

Every pump is paid by the height and the volume.

A pump raises the pressure of a fluid. Lifting water by a height h needs a pressure of ρgh, and the power is that pressure times the flow. For water, each metre of lift costs 9.81 kJ per m³ (using standard gravity, NIST), about 2.7 Wh.

Worked: the 10.33 m suction limit

The atmosphere can hold up a water column of h = P ÷ (ρ g) = 101,325 ÷ (1,000 × 9.80665) ≈ 10.33 m (NIST, NIST). Real suction pumps manage less, because water’s own vapour pressure and friction eat into it (see Boil). Torricelli’s 1644 mercury barometer is the same experiment with a denser liquid (Museo Galileo).

Working: the drone figure

RAND’s baseline design needs about 1.5 kWh to deliver a 5 lb (2.27 kg) payload out to a 10-mile (16.1 km) radius. 2.27 kg × 16.1 km = 0.0365 tonne-km; 1.5 kWh ÷ 0.0365 ≈ 41 kWh ≈ 148 MJ per tonne-km, counted on the one-way distance. Energies 2022 MJ figures are converted from its per-100-tonne-km values. NITI and RITES costs are for different years, so compare within a column, not across sources.

Two takeaways

  • Energy per tonne-km spans about 1,500 times, from a ship (0.099 MJ) to a delivery drone (≈150 MJ, our estimate). Drones only pay where value per kilogram or speed is very high.
  • Cheapest in India is not road. Both cited studies price rail below road per tonne-km. Road wins on door-to-door speed and flexibility, which matter most for perishables.
03

The climb

The same idea scales from 5 litres to 622 cubic metres a second.

  • The body. The heart beats around 100,000 times a day and pumps about 5 litres of blood around the body (BHF).
  • The city. Delhi Jal Board charges households by the kilolitre, in slabs, for water it pumps and delivers (DJB).
  • Industry and farming. The Gayatri pumphouse in Telangana, part of the Kaleshwaram scheme, lifts water 118 m with seven 139 MW units, 622 m³ per second, at least 2 TMC ft a day. It sits 178 m underground and was built in under 42 months, according to its builder MEIL (MEIL).
  • Rockets. An F-1 engine’s turbopump moved about 42,500 gallons of propellant a minute. The one at the Smithsonian was recovered from the ocean floor in 2013, from the Saturn V that launched Apollo 11 (Smithsonian NASM). See Launch.
  • India in space. A sourced ISRO turbopump figure is TBC.
04

The record, and how far it goes

A river lifted higher than a 30-storey building.

Claim: MEIL describes the Gayatri pumphouse as the “largest irrigation pumping station in the world”, at 973 MW installed (MEIL). This is the builder’s claim; an independent ranking is TBC.

Worked: distance from normal

At normal sea-level pressure, suction stops at 10.33 m. Gayatri pushes water 118 m: 118 ÷ 10.33 ≈ 11.4 times the suction limit. Flow: one F-1 turbopump moved 42,500 US gal × 3.785 L ≈ 161,000 L per minute ≈ 2.7 m³/s; Gayatri moves 622 m³/s, 622 ÷ 2.7 ≈ 230 times more.

05

Try it: find the suction limit with a straw

Feel the atmosphere doing the lifting.

You need: a glass of water, two or three straws, and tape.

  1. Drink through one straw: easy.
  2. Tape straws end to end to make a long one and try again, standing up. It gets harder, because you must lower the pressure in your mouth further.
  3. Pinch the straw’s top shut, lift it out, and watch the water stay in: the air below holds it up.
  4. Work out: how long a straw would stop working completely? (About 10 m, the worked limit above.)

Safety

Do not climb on furniture or stairs with a straw. Use clean water you are happy to drink.

06

Limits: what stops us

Pumps fight the same three enemies at every scale.

  • Suction stops at about 10 m, less in practice and less at altitude, where air pressure is lower.
  • Friction in pipes adds to the head a pump must supply; long pipes need more power. Exact losses are design-specific (TBC).
  • Cavitation. If pressure at the inlet drops to the vapour pressure, the water boils into bubbles that collapse and damage the pump (see Boil).
  • Efficiency. Even a new giant station loses about a quarter of its input (our estimate above).
02 · Distance from normal

How far the records go from normal

Pump: from a 10.33 m suction limit to a 118 m river lift

On a normal sea-level day, air pressure can lift water no higher than 10.33 m. Pushing pumps go far beyond it: the Gayatri pumphouse lifts a river 118 m, about 11 times the suction limit, at 622 m³ a second, about 230 times the flow of a Saturn V F-1 turbopump.

03 · Review

Check your reasoning

Answer each one in your notebook before you open it.

Why does a hand pump fail on a deep borewell?

Suction relies on air pressure, which can hold up only about 10.33 m of water.

How much energy, at least, does it take to lift 1 m³ of water 118 m?

About 1.16 MJ, or 0.32 kWh, from ρgh.

Why is the Delhi water illustration not a real value-per-kWh figure?

The price and the lift energy belong to different water; Delhi’s supply is not lifted by Gayatri.

What is cavitation?

Water boiling into bubbles at a pump inlet where pressure falls to its vapour pressure; the bubbles collapse and erode the pump.
