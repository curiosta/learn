Control · Regulate · Boil

Why doesn’t water always boil at 100 °C?

Boiling is a contest between a liquid’s own vapour pressure and the pressure pushing down on it. Control the pressure and you control the boiling point, from a hill-station cup of tea to a power-station boiler to a rocket’s fuel tank.

Start here: tea at a hill station

Make tea in Leh or Shimla and it never tastes quite as strong; dal and rice take longer to cook. In a pressure cooker the same dal cooks in minutes. The flame is the same. What changed?

Pause and answer
  1. What has to happen inside water for a bubble of steam to form and survive?
  2. How would the air pressing down on the pot affect that?
  3. What does a pressure cooker’s weight on the vent do?
Compare your reasoning

A steam bubble forms when the water’s own vapour pressure matches the pressure pushing on it. Lower the outside pressure (high altitude) and the water boils sooner, at a lower temperature, so food cooks in cooler water. A pressure cooker traps steam so the pressure inside rises, and the water must get hotter, about 120 °C, before it can boil. The boiling point is a pressure reading.

What you can do with this

  • Home habit: turn the flame down once it boils

    Row 1: once water boils, extra heat goes into making steam (0.63 kWh per kg), not into hotter water. A lid and a lower flame keep the boil while wasting less. How much fuel this saves in your kitchen is TBC: compare gas-cylinder days.

01

The physics in plain words

Boiling is when a liquid can push back as hard as the air.

Every liquid gives off vapour. The hotter it is, the harder that vapour pushes: water’s vapour pressure is only about 3.17 kPa at 25 °C but reaches 101.325 kPa at 99.97 °C (NIST WebBook). Boiling starts when the two pressures match. While it boils, extra heat goes into breaking molecules free, the latent heat, not into warming: about 2,256 kJ per kg at 100 °C (NIST).

Worked: boiling dry costs seven times the heating

Warming 1 kg from 25 to 100 °C: about 314 kJ (see Heat). Boiling it all away: about 2,256 kJ. 2,256 ÷ 314 ≈ 7.2. That is why a pot takes so much longer to boil dry than to come to the boil.

03

The climb

Boiling drove the industrial revolution and still fuels rockets.

  • Steam engines. James Watt’s patent of 5 January 1769 added a separate condenser: condensing the steam somewhere cold rather than cooling the whole cylinder each stroke, which saved most of the wasted fuel (Science Museum).
  • Power stations. Coal and nuclear plants still boil water at high pressure, 2 to 15 MPa and above, then spin turbines with the steam (table above). See Convert.
  • Rockets. Liquid hydrogen boils at 20.37 K and liquid oxygen at about 90 K (NIST); their tanks boil off constantly. ISRO’s indigenous cryogenic upper stage first flew on GSLV-D5 on 5 January 2014 (ISRO).
  • The body. At about 19 km altitude, “Armstrong’s line” near 63,000 ft, pressure falls to the point that water boils at body temperature; body fluids boil without a pressure suit (FAA). NIST’s table puts water’s vapour pressure at 37 °C at 6.28 kPa.
04

The record, and how far it goes

The coldest-boiling liquid needed its own century-long chase.

Record: helium is the liquid with the lowest boiling point, 4.22 K (−268.93 °C) at one atmosphere (NIST). Heike Kamerlingh Onnes first liquefied it at Leiden on 10 July 1908 (Leiden University). Liquid helium let him discover superconductivity in 1911 (Physics Today), and it still cools CERN’s magnets today (see Cool).

Worked: distance from normal

Water boils at 373.12 K at one atmosphere; helium at 4.22 K. 373.12 ÷ 4.22 ≈ 88: helium boils about 88 times colder, in kelvin, than water. Against Earth’s normal 288 K, helium boils at 288 ÷ 4.22 ≈ 68 times colder than normal.

Hot end: no boiling at all

Above water’s critical point, 373.95 °C and 22.064 MPa, there is no boiling: liquid and vapour become a single fluid. “Supercritical” power plants run beyond it.

05

Try it: boil water without a flame

Lower the pressure and watch warm water boil.

You need: a large plastic syringe without a needle (from a pharmacy), warm tap water (not hot), and a fingertip.

  1. Draw in a little warm water, push out the air, and seal the tip firmly with your finger.
  2. Pull the plunger back hard.
  3. Bubbles appear and the water “boils”, though it is barely warm. You have lowered the pressure on it below its vapour pressure.
  4. Let go of the plunger: the bubbles vanish.

Safety

Use warm tap water only, never boiling water. Keep the syringe pointed away from your face.

06

Limits: what stops us

Controlling boiling means controlling pressure, and pressure must be held.

  • Vessels must hold the pressure. Boilers and cookers need safety valves; see Pressurise under Contain.
  • Cryogenic fuel boils away. Heat leaking into a tank boils off fuel, so cryogenic rockets are topped up until just before launch and insulated heavily (Insulate is planned).
  • The body has no pressure valve. Above Armstrong’s line the only protection is a pressure suit or cabin.
02 · Distance from normal

How far the records go from normal

Boil: from 72 °C on Everest to 4.22 K for helium

On a normal sea-level day water boils at 99.97 °C. Nature moves that by only a few degrees with the weather and by about 28 °C on the top of Everest. Engineering pushes water’s boiling point to 342 °C in high-pressure boilers, and past the critical point at 374 °C boiling disappears. The coldest-boiling liquid, helium, boils at 4.22 K, about 68 times colder than normal.

03 · Review

Check your reasoning

Answer each one in your notebook before you open it.

Why does tea brewed on a mountain taste weaker?

The water boils at a lower temperature (about 72 °C on Everest), so it cannot extract as much from the leaves.

A pot has been boiling for ten minutes. Is the water hotter than when it first boiled?

No. While it boils at constant pressure, extra heat goes into latent heat of vaporisation; the temperature stays at the boiling point.

Why does it take about seven times longer to boil a pot dry than to bring it to the boil?

Vaporising 1 kg takes about 2,256 kJ; warming it from 25 to 100 °C takes about 314 kJ.

Why must a pilot above 63,000 ft wear a pressure suit even in a warm cabin?

Outside pressure is so low that water boils at body temperature; if the cabin fails, body fluids would boil.
