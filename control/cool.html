Control · Regulate · Cool

How cold can we make things?

You cannot pour cold in. Cooling means carrying heat out, uphill, from a cold place to a warmer one, and it gets harder with every degree. From your own sweat to 38 trillionths of a degree above absolute zero.

Start here: sweat and a matka

On a hot afternoon you sweat, and a breeze feels wonderful. A clay matka keeps drinking water cooler than the room though nothing inside it is cold. Neither has a motor. Where does the heat go?

Pause and answer
  1. What does water need in order to evaporate?
  2. Where does the energy for evaporation come from?
  3. Why might a humid day make sweat feel useless?
Compare your reasoning

To turn liquid water into vapour, molecules must break free of their neighbours, and that costs energy. Evaporating sweat takes it from your skin; water seeping through the matka’s walls takes it from the water inside. At skin temperature each gram that evaporates carries off about 2.4 kJ (NIST). On a humid day the air is already loaded with vapour, evaporation slows, and the cooling fails. That is the wet-bulb limit on Normal on Earth. A fridge does the same trick in a closed loop, with a pump to make it work in any weather.

Where the value is

Where does cooling pay best per unit of energy?

Cooling rarely changes what matter is. It mostly holds matter: a potato kept firm until prices recover, oxygen kept liquid until a hospital needs it. Below we rank three cooling processes by value added per kilowatt-hour. Value added means the later or delivered price minus the starting price and the storage charges. Every figure is dated.

Where this sits on the value quadrant: Time mainly. Cold storage holds matter until a later, higher price, and cold chains add Space by letting matter travel to where demand is. See the quadrant.

What you can do with this

  • Small business: know your break-even before you store

    Row 1 only pays if the later price clears the purchase price, the rent and the loss. Using the figures above, break-even was about ₹1,255 per quintal. A farmer group or trader can redo the sum each season with its own mandi price and rent before booking space. A store’s fixed costs are spread over every bag, which is why utilisation matters (next card).

  • Home habit: test the “full store” rule on your own freezer

    Singh et al. found that each extra 0.1 of capacity utilisation cut energy use by about 1.46 kWh per tonne (row 1). If your home has a separate electricity meter reading, compare a week with a near-empty freezer against a week with a fuller one. Whether the effect is large at home is TBC: measure it rather than assume it.

  • Skill: refrigeration servicing

    Every row here depends on compressors, insulation and seals kept in good order. Servicing skills keep a cold store’s variable cost down. They also travel well, from a village cold store to a hospital oxygen plant.

01

The physics in plain words

Every cooler is a heat pump: it moves heat from cold to hot, and pays for it.

Heat flows by itself only from hot to cold. To make something colder than its surroundings you must carry heat out against that flow, which costs work. Three main tricks:

  • Evaporation. A liquid boiling or evaporating soaks up its latent heat: about 2,413 kJ/kg for water at 37 °C and 2,256 kJ/kg at 100 °C (NIST WebBook).
  • Vapour compression. Evaporate a refrigerant inside the fridge, compress the vapour outside so it condenses and dumps the heat into the room, and repeat. Jacob Perkins patented this cycle on 14 August 1834 (ASME).
  • Expansion and lasers. Gases cool as they expand through a valve or turbine; that is how hydrogen and helium were first liquefied. Lasers and magnetic traps take atoms the rest of the way to nanokelvin and below.
02

A worked fridge

Physics caps how much heat each joule of work can move.

Worked: the best any fridge could do

The ideal (Carnot) coefficient of performance is COP = T_cold ÷ (T_hot − T_cold), in kelvin.

Fridge at 4 °C (277 K) in a 35 °C kitchen (308 K): 277 ÷ 31 ≈ 8.9. At best, each joule of electricity moves about 9 joules of heat out.

Now cool to 1.9 K, the LHC’s magnets, rejecting heat at about 300 K: 1.9 ÷ 298 ≈ 0.0064. Even a perfect machine needs about 160 joules of work per joule removed.

That steep fall explains why CERN’s cryogenics needs 40 MW of electricity and 120 tonnes of helium to hold the magnets at 1.9 K (CERN).

04

The record, and how far it goes

Thirty-eight trillionths of a degree, reached by dropping atoms down a tower.

Record: a German team (Hannover, Bremen’s ZARM drop-tower institute, Mainz, Berlin) lowered the internal kinetic energy of a Bose–Einstein condensate of about 101,000 atoms to the equivalent of 38 picokelvin (+6/−7 pK), published in Physical Review Letters on 30 August 2021 (Deppner et al., PRL 127, 100401). They did it by combining a magnetic “lens” with an oscillation of the condensate itself, so the cloud barely spreads; the method is built for long free-fall experiments.

Worked: distance from normal

288 K ÷ 38 × 10⁻¹² K ≈ 7.6 × 10¹². The record is about 7.6 trillion times colder than Earth’s normal surface, from absolute zero. Nature’s coldest surface reading, Vostok’s 184 K, is 0.64 times normal.

What this record does and doesn’t mean

The 38 pK figure describes the spread of atom velocities along certain directions, an effective kinetic temperature, not a whole object sitting in a fridge. It is stated as “3/2 kB·38 pK” of kinetic energy in three dimensions.

05

Try it: feel evaporation

Build the instrument that measures wet-bulb temperature.

You need: two kitchen or aquarium thermometers, a scrap of cotton cloth, water, and a fan or a breezy window.

  1. Wrap one thermometer bulb in damp cloth. Leave the other bare.
  2. Put both in the breeze for five minutes and read them.
  3. The difference is evaporative cooling at work. The wet reading is the wet-bulb temperature.
  4. Repeat on a humid day and a dry day. When is the gap smallest? What does that mean for sweating?
06

Limits: what stops us

You can approach zero kelvin but never reach it.

  • Absolute zero is a floor, not a target. The kelvin scale starts there (BIPM); every halving of temperature costs more work than the last (see the Carnot sum).
  • Heat leaks in. Cryogenic tanks and magnets need vacuum jackets and multilayer insulation; see Contain.
  • The body’s limit is humid heat. Sweat stops working near a wet-bulb temperature of 35 °C in theory (Sherwood & Huber) and around 31 °C in young adults tested in humid heat (Vecellio et al.).
02 · Distance from normal

How far the records go from normal

Cool: about 7.6 trillion times colder than normal

Normal is 288 K. Nature’s coldest surface air reached 0.64 times that. The LHC holds 36,000 tonnes of magnet mass at 1.9 K, about 150 times colder than normal (worked: 288 ÷ 1.9). The coldest atoms made, 38 pK in 2021, are about 7.6 × 10¹² times colder.

03 · Review

Check your reasoning

Answer each one in your notebook before you open it.

Why does a fridge’s back feel warm?

It is dumping the heat it pulled from inside, plus the electrical work that drove the compressor, into the room.

Why does cooling to 1.9 K need so much more power than cooling to 4 °C?

The Carnot COP falls with T_cold ÷ (T_hot − T_cold). At 277 K it is about 8.9; at 1.9 K it is about 0.006, so each joule removed costs over 1,000 times more work.

A matka works well in Jaipur in May, poorly in Mumbai in July. Why?

Dry air lets water evaporate fast through the clay; humid air slows evaporation, so less heat is carried off.

Why do rockets use liquid hydrogen if it must be kept at 20 K?

Hydrogen burned with oxygen gives high exhaust speed per kilogram of propellant; the price is cryogenic tanks and insulation.
