Control map / Move

Control · General verb · Move

How fast and how far can we move things?

Moving means pushing mass against friction, air and gravity. From a bucket of water to a train, a plane, a rocket, and the fastest object ever built.

Start here: carrying a bucket upstairs

Carry a 10 kg bucket of water up one floor. Then carry it the same distance along a flat corridor. Your legs say the stairs were harder. Physically, what is different?

Pause and answer
  1. What force are you working against on the stairs?
  2. What are you working against on the flat?
  3. How would wheels change each case?
Compare your reasoning

On the stairs you push against gravity: lifting 10 kg by 3 m stores about 294 J. On the flat, ideally, you do no work against gravity at all; what tires you is your own body’s motion and friction. Wheels cut friction on the flat, which is why carts and trains moved goods long before anything lifted them into the sky. Every Move verb fights one of three things: gravity, friction or air.

01

The everyday case, worked

Gravity sets the cost of going up; friction and air set the cost of going along.

Worked: lifting a bucket

Energy = m × g × h = 10 kg × 9.81 m/s² × 3 m ≈ 294 J (g from NIST). That is about what a kettle (assumed 2 kW) delivers in 0.15 s. Lifting is cheap; doing it fast and often is what needs engines.

02

The mechanisms

Each new way of moving cut one of the three resistances.

  • Wheels and rails cut friction. Steel wheels on steel rails roll very easily.
  • Wings trade forward speed for lift, so a plane holds its weight up on moving air.
  • Magnetic levitation removes wheel contact altogether.
  • Rockets need no air at all: they push on their own exhaust (see Launch).

04

The record, and how far it goes

The fastest object people have made was built to fall towards the Sun.

Record: Parker Solar Probe reached about 430,000 mph (≈192 km/s) on 24 December 2024 (NASA).

Worked: distance from normal

Against Earth’s escape velocity: 192 ÷ 11.186 ≈ 17. Against the fastest train: 692,000 km/h ÷ 603 km/h ≈ 1,150. Full story on Launch.

05

Limits

The faster you go through air, the hotter and harder it gets.

  • Air resistance rises steeply with speed, which is why record trains are streamlined and record speeds are set in space.
  • Heat: fast flight through air heats the craft (see Heat).
  • People: crew need pressure, oxygen and limited g-forces (see Normal on Earth).
02 · Distance from normal

How far the records go from normal

Move: about 17 times escape velocity

On a normal day, a person walks at a few km/h and nothing thrown leaves Earth. The fastest train runs at 603 km/h; Parker Solar Probe at about 692,000 km/h, some 17 times Earth’s escape velocity of 11.186 km/s.

03 · Review

Check your reasoning

Answer each one in your notebook before you open it.

Why is carrying a bucket upstairs harder than along a corridor?

On the stairs you do work against gravity (m × g × h); on the flat, ideally, none.

Why do maglev trains go faster than wheeled ones?

They remove wheel contact and its friction and wear; air resistance becomes the main limit.

Why can a rocket work in space where a plane cannot?

A plane needs air to make lift and to burn its fuel; a rocket carries its oxidiser and pushes on its own exhaust.
