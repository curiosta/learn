Career · lead time

The aiming diagram.

Drag your position on the timeline and watch predictability, your arrow, the baby's arrow, the hedge, and the retraining cost curve recalculate. Below: when each compounding skill pays off across a lifespan.

now · age 30
The core trade-off: distance buys ambition, but it buys fog. Every extra year of lead time multiplies the chance the world you aimed at is not the world that arrives. The counterweight is compounding — each year of practiced judgment makes next year's target more predictable for you personally, even as the world's predictability decays.
Five layers

What the diagram carries

  • Your arrow

    A near target, about five years out

    Close enough that your current moat still reaches it. Far enough that it is not just today's job with a new label.

  • Baby's arrow

    A far target, about fifteen years out

    A newborn has twenty years of runway, so the far target is cheap to aim at — but it sits deep in the unpredictable zone, where almost nothing is knowable yet.

  • Feedback loop

    Skills compound; predictability moves with you

    Practice produces judgment; judgment produces better practice. Your personal predictable zone creeps forward even as the world's decays.

  • Moat

    Your current position constrains the reachable set

    A designer aiming at AI-assisted design has a shorter, more reliable arrow than one aiming at pure engineering. Not every future is in range from where you stand.

  • Split arrow

    Primary bet plus a dashed hedge

    With a short runway you cannot afford one wrong bet. Keep two arrows alive: one solid commitment, one dashed option that costs little to maintain.

  • Cost curve

    Retraining is not linear

    Year one of learning something new is cheap. Year three is expensive. Past a point you are not upskilling — you are starting over.

Lifespan

When each skill pays off

The eight compounding skills are not equally urgent at every age. Each one has a window where it compounds hardest — and a cost to starting late.

Ages 15–25 · foundation

Meta-learning & taste

The cheapest years to learn how to learn, and the most formative for taste — every project recalibrates what you notice. Starting late here means relearning the habit of learning.

meta-learningtaste
Ages 22–35 · building

Judgment & writing

Judgment compounds from the first decision you own; writing compounds from the first piece you publish. Both need volume, and volume needs runway. This is the window where the cost curve is still flat.

judgmentwriting
Ages 30–45 · compounding

Distribution & relationships

Audience and trust take a decade to build and cannot be rushed. This is when the moat is deepest — and when the hedge matters most, because one wrong bet costs the most.

distributionrelationships
Ages 40–60 · teaching

Teaching & systems thinking

Teaching forces mastery and builds the tribe at the same time. Systems thinking tells you where the arrow lands. Both pay off hardest when you have something worth explaining.

teachingsystems thinking
