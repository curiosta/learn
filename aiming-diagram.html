Career · lead time

The aiming diagram.

The world moves left to right. You stand somewhere on it. The farther ahead you aim, the less predictable the world gets — but your skills pull predictability forward with you.

The core trade-off: distance buys ambition, but it buys fog. Every extra year of lead time multiplies the chance the world you aimed at is not the world that arrives. The counterweight is compounding — each year of practiced judgment makes next year's target more predictable for you personally, even as the world's predictability decays.
Five layers

What the diagram carries

  • Your arrow

    A near target, about five years out

    Close enough that your current moat still reaches it. Far enough that it is not just today's job with a new label.

  • Baby's arrow

    A far target, about fifteen years out

    A newborn has twenty years of runway, so the far target is cheap to aim at — but it sits deep in the unpredictable zone, where almost nothing is knowable yet.

  • Feedback loop

    Skills compound; predictability moves with you

    Practice produces judgment; judgment produces better practice. Your personal predictable zone creeps forward even as the world's decays. That is the real advantage of being mid-career, not a fixed starting point.

  • Moat

    Your current position constrains the reachable set

    A designer aiming at AI-assisted design has a shorter, more reliable arrow than one aiming at pure engineering. Not every future is in range from where you stand.

  • Split arrow

    Primary bet plus a dashed hedge

    With a short runway you cannot afford one wrong bet. Keep two arrows alive: one solid commitment, one dashed option that costs little to maintain.

  • Cost curve

    Retraining is not linear

    Year one of learning something new is cheap. Year three is expensive. Past a point you are not upskilling — you are starting over, and the cost exceeds the value of the new skill.
